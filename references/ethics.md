# The rail

Not a disclaimer bolted onto a persuasion skill. The rail is derived from two findings in the same corpus that produces the technique.

## Why a rail is obligatory

**Green & Brock (2000), Experiment 2: highly transported readers detected fewer false notes in a story.** Immersion does not just hold attention. It measurably lowers the reader's scrutiny of what they are being told.

That is the whole argument. A skill that teaches immersion is a skill that teaches lowering a reader's defenses. There is no version of this that is ethically neutral, so the rail is part of the technique rather than an afterthought.

## Why dishonesty is also bad craft

The commercial case runs the same direction as the ethical one, which is convenient but not the reason.

Clickbait raises clicks and damages trust. Overhyped promises that do not deliver produce distrust and defiance in readers, and that defiance impedes the sharing the clickbait was written to get. Readers increasingly form beliefs about a publisher's intent, and those beliefs persist past the individual piece.

So an unclosed gap is not a clever technique with an ethical cost. It is a technique that stops working, on a timescale slightly longer than the one most people measure.

## The four rules

### 1. Every gap you open, you close, inside the piece

This is the definition of the line between a hook and clickbait. Not tone. Not intensity. Whether the promise is kept in the same artifact that made it.

A gap closed in a follow-up post, behind an email signup, or in a paid product is not closed. If you open it, you owe it.

Applies at every scale: a paragraph that opens a slot owes its closure to a later paragraph, and the piece as a whole owes closure by its ending.

### 2. No claim the piece cannot support

Name the source or cut the claim. "Studies show," "experts agree," "research suggests," and "it is widely known" are all ways of borrowing authority you have not earned.

If the writer has no source, ask for one. Do not supply one from memory unless you can name it precisely enough for the reader to check it. If you can only half-remember it, say so in the text or leave it out.

Tag uncertainty rather than hiding it. `evidence.md` has a section for findings encountered secondhand, and it exists because two figures in this skill's own research could not be traced to a primary source. That is the standard.

### 3. Vivid evidence, never omitted evidence

Because immersion suppresses counterargument, the writer holds an advantage the reader cannot see. The rule that follows: **supply the counterargument rather than profiting from its absence.**

This is not balance for its own sake. It is specific. If the strongest objection to your claim is one the reader would raise if they were not transported, you name it. You are allowed to answer it. You are not allowed to rely on the fact that they will not think of it.

Practically: the honest limit goes in the piece. This skill's own SKILL.md states its effect sizes and says the procedure is a construction. That is rule 3 applied to itself.

### 4. Concreteness must be true. Blocking rule.

**Never invent a name, number, date, quote, statistic, or mechanism.**

This is the rule that matters most for an AI writing prose, and it deserves the space.

Peitho rewards concreteness. Every paragraph is supposed to carry an anchor: a name, a number, a date, a mechanism. That creates direct pressure to produce a specific when none is available. And a fabricated specific is the worst possible output, because concreteness is exactly what makes prose persuasive. The invented detail is *more* convincing than the vague truth it replaced.

So the pressure this skill creates points straight at its own worst failure. Hence: blocking, not advisory.

When you need an anchor and do not have one:

- Ask for the real number.
- Use a real number you can cite, even if it is less impressive.
- Write around it. "Deploy time dropped by more than half" is weaker than "from 40 minutes to 4" and infinitely better than inventing "from 40 minutes to 4."
- Mark the slot. `[NUMBER NEEDED]` in a draft is honest. A plausible invention is not.

**Placeholder names, sample data, and fiction are exempt** in the obvious way: a short story's characters are not fabricated evidence, and neither is example data labeled as example data. The rule governs claims presented as true.

## Aggression is disclosed, not blocked

Hard-edged copy is legitimate. Strong opinions, blunt language, unflattering comparisons, direct challenges to the reader. Write them when asked.

The obligation is transparency about what was written, not softening. Say plainly that a draft is aggressive, name what it risks, and let the decision belong to the person publishing it.

## What the rail does not do

It does not require hedging. It does not require both-sidesing every claim. It does not require softening a real conclusion into a maybe. Rules 2 and 3 are about sourcing and about naming the strongest objection, not about diluting the argument.

A piece can be certain, sharp, and one-sided and still pass all four rules, provided every claim is supported, every gap closes, the best objection is named, and no detail was invented.
