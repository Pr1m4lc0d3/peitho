# Worked specimens

Openings taken apart step by step so they can be imitated rather than guessed at.

**How to use this file:** find the specimen whose form and audience stance match yours. Read the *What to steal* line. Apply that move to your material. Do not copy the phrasing.

**On accuracy:** quotes marked *verbatim* are ones I can state with confidence. Quotes marked *paraphrase* are described rather than quoted because I cannot verify the exact wording, which is ethics rail rule 4 applied to this file. Translated works name the situation. If you need a quote for publication, check it against a primary source.

Format names in brackets come from Heritage & Greatbatch's seven measured applause devices, or from classical rhetoric.

---

## 1. Tolstoy, *Anna Karenina* (Garnett translation)

> "Happy families are all alike; every unhappy family is unhappy in its own way." *(verbatim, translation)*

**Format:** contrast. The single strongest device in Heritage & Greatbatch, at 33.2% of collective applause.

**Stance:** receptive. Reader has opened a novel.

**Exordium jobs:** attentive (a claim about their own family, immediately), receptive (it is a proposition, and propositions invite agreement or dispute), well disposed (it treats the reader as capable of a general idea).

**The gap:** if unhappiness is where the variety lives, then this book is about a specific unhappiness. Which one? Visible, unanswered, and the reader can name it.

**What to steal:** open on a genuine contrast where both halves carry weight. Not a strawman negation. Test it: would a reasonable person defend the side you are rejecting? If not, you have the banned fake-contrast pattern instead.

## 2. García Márquez, *One Hundred Years of Solitude* (Rabassa translation)

> "Many years later, as he faced the firing squad, Colonel Aureliano Buendía was to remember that distant afternoon when his father took him to discover ice." *(verbatim, translation)*

**Format:** headline-punchline, running backward.

**Stance:** receptive.

**The gap:** three time frames in one sentence, and none of them explained. But note what is *given away*: the colonel ends up in front of a firing squad. The outcome is spoiled in the first eleven words.

**Why this is the most important specimen in the file:** it is a direct refutation of "withhold the outcome to build suspense." Leavitt & Christenfeld found spoiled stories were enjoyed more across three genres. This sentence does it on purpose. Knowing the destination is what creates the pull, because now the only question is *how*, and *how* is the whole book.

**What to steal:** give away the outcome and keep the mechanism. Works in marketing too: "This cut our deploy time to four minutes. It took three failed attempts to get there."

## 3. Cicero, First Catilinarian

> "Quo usque tandem abutere, Catilina, patientia nostra?" / "How long, Catiline, will you go on abusing our patience?" *(verbatim Latin; English rendering varies)*

**Format:** puzzle-solution, opened with a question. Also *pursuit* in the Heritage & Greatbatch scheme: the speaker presses a point the audience already half-holds.

**Stance:** the Senate is nervous, not hostile. Cicero needs urgency, not permission.

**Exordium jobs:** attentive (a direct address to a man in the room), receptive (the question presupposes the abuse, so answering it means conceding it), well disposed (*nostra*, "our" patience, puts speaker and Senate on one side).

**The gap:** the accusation is asserted, not proved. The reader knows exactly what is missing: the evidence. That is what makes them stay for it.

**What to steal:** a question whose premise does the argument. This is the enthymeme at work. The audience supplies the missing conviction. It is also the move most easily abused, so rule 2 of the rail applies hard: only presuppose what you can then support.

## 4. Orwell, *Politics and the English Language*

> "Most people who bother with the matter at all would admit that the English language is in a bad way, but it is generally assumed that we cannot by conscious action do anything about it." *(verbatim)*

**Format:** *insinuatio*, the oblique approach, plus contrast.

**Stance:** hostile or resigned. Orwell is about to tell writers they write badly.

**How the obliqueness works:** he concedes the reader's likely position first (yes, the language is in a bad way, everyone knows), then names the resignation, and attacks *the resignation* rather than the reader. By the time the demand arrives, the reader has already agreed to two things.

**The gap:** if conscious action *can* do something, what is it? The essay is the answer.

**What to steal:** when your reader will resist the claim, open on what they already accept, then attack the fatalism rather than the person. This is the correct opening for critical marketing copy, for a post telling an industry it is wrong, and for any piece whose thesis is "you are doing this badly."

## 5. Lincoln, Gettysburg Address

> "Four score and seven years ago our fathers brought forth on this continent, a new nation, conceived in Liberty, and dedicated to the proposition that all men are created equal." *(verbatim)*

**Format:** position taking. The speaker states where he stands before doing anything else.

**Stance:** grieving, exhausted, and standing in a cemetery. Wearied by listening in Cicero's exact third sense: Edward Everett had already spoken for around two hours.

**Exordium jobs:** attentive (a date, counted oddly, which forces the listener to compute), receptive (frames the war as a test of a proposition rather than a conflict of armies), well disposed (*our* fathers).

**The gap:** the nation was dedicated to a proposition. Propositions can fail. Is this one failing? Two hundred and seventy-two words then answer it.

**What to steal:** "four score and seven" instead of "eighty-seven" is not decoration, it is friction that buys attention at the exact moment attention is lowest. When your reader is exhausted, a small deliberate strangeness outperforms volume.

## 6. Frederick Douglass, "What to the Slave Is the Fourth of July?" (1852)

Douglass opens by describing his own nervousness at addressing the audience, and his sense of inadequacy to the occasion, before turning to the indictment. *(paraphrase; I cannot verify the exact wording, do not quote from this file)*

**Format:** *captatio benevolentiae* into *insinuatio*.

**Stance:** maximally hostile to the argument that is coming, and Douglass knows it. He is about to tell a room of celebrating Americans that their holiday is a fraud.

**How it works:** he spends the opening lowering himself and honoring the audience. The goodwill is banked before the withdrawal. The indictment, when it lands, lands on people who have already accepted him as sincere.

**What to steal:** when the thesis will offend, buy goodwill first and spend it later. Never spend it first. The order is not politeness, it is load-bearing.

## 7. Emmeline Pankhurst, "Freedom or Death" (Hartford, 1913)

Pankhurst opens by refusing the role of advocate, and identifies herself instead as a person whom the courts of her own country have declared dangerous. *(paraphrase; wording unverified)*

**Format:** headline-punchline.

**Stance:** curious, skeptical, foreign audience.

**How it works:** she declines the expected frame (persuade us) and substitutes a harder one (I am already a convict). The self-description is the concrete anchor, and it is a fact rather than an adjective.

**What to steal:** refuse the frame the reader expects, and replace it with a verifiable fact about yourself that is stranger than the frame. Works in a bio, an about page, and a cold email.

## 8. Kafka, *The Metamorphosis* (Muir translation)

> "As Gregor Samsa awoke one morning from uneasy dreams he found himself transformed in his bed into a gigantic insect." *(verbatim, translation)*

**Format:** headline-punchline. The whole event, delivered flat.

**The gap:** everything about *how* and *why*, none of it acknowledged. The prose is calm, and the calm is what opens the slot. If the sentence were alarmed, the reader would have nothing to do.

**What to steal:** deliver the extraordinary fact in ordinary syntax. Understatement transfers the reaction to the reader, which is participation. Overstatement performs the reaction for them, which is not.

---

## 9. Camus, *The Stranger* (Gilbert translation)

> "Mother died today. Or maybe yesterday; I can't be sure." *(verbatim, translation)*

**Format:** headline-punchline, then immediate destabilization.

**The gap:** not "what happened." It is "what is wrong with this narrator." The second sentence is what creates it, and it does so by retracting the certainty of the first.

**What to steal:** state, then undercut. The undercut is the slot. Two sentences, and the reader is already forming a judgment they will spend the book testing.

---

## 10. Ogilvy, Rolls-Royce advertisement (1958)

> "At 60 miles an hour the loudest noise in this new Rolls-Royce comes from the electric clock." *(verbatim)*

**Format:** headline-punchline. Also the cleanest demonstration of concreteness in the file.

**Stance:** hostile by default. It is an advertisement.

**Why it works:** every word is checkable. A speed, a specific noise, a specific component. No adjective does any work. "Whisper-quiet luxury" makes the same claim and carries none of the force, because the reader can dispute an adjective and cannot dispute a clock.

**Concreteness calibration:** this sits at the top of the inverted U. One more specific detail would start reading as a spec sheet, which is the over-concrete tail the 8,977-headline meta-analysis identified.

**What to steal:** replace every evaluative adjective with a fact a skeptic could verify. If no such fact exists, you do not yet have the claim, and rule 4 of the rail forbids inventing one.

## 11. Sojourner Truth, "Ain't I a Woman?" (1851): a deliberate non-specimen

This speech is famous and I am not quoting it, because two substantially different transcriptions exist: a contemporaneous 1851 report and Frances Gage's considerably altered 1863 version, which is the one usually reproduced, dialect and refrain included.

It is here as the demonstration case for rule 2 of the rail. The most quoted version is probably not what was said. Anyone citing it confidently is borrowing authority they have not checked.

**What to steal:** the discipline. When a source is contested, say it is contested. That sentence costs you nothing and is the difference between a writer a reader can trust and one they cannot.

## Pattern summary

| Your situation | Specimen | The move |
|---|---|---|
| Reader will resist the thesis | Orwell, Douglass | Concede first, attack the resignation, spend goodwill later |
| Reader is exhausted | Lincoln | Small deliberate friction, not more volume |
| You have a strong outcome to report | García Márquez | Give away the outcome, keep the mechanism |
| You have a real number | Ogilvy | Cut every adjective, keep the checkable fact |
| You have two substantive sides | Tolstoy | True contrast, both halves weighted |
| Something extraordinary happened | Kafka | Flat syntax, let the reader react |
| Your credibility is the story | Pankhurst | Refuse the expected frame, substitute a verifiable fact |
| You need urgency | Cicero | A question whose premise carries the argument |
| Your source is contested | Sojourner Truth | Say so |
