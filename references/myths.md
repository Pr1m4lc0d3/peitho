# Buried advice

Five widely repeated pieces of hook and engagement advice that the evidence contradicts. Each entry names what killed it and what to do instead.

This file exists because most writing advice in circulation is craft lore repeated confidently. Lore is allowed in Peitho, tagged `[L]`. Lore dressed as science is not.

---

## 1. "Withhold the outcome to create suspense"

**Killed by:** Leavitt & Christenfeld (2011), *Psychological Science*. Three experiments, 12 short stories, three genres (ironic-twist, mystery, literary). Readers enjoyed the **spoiled** versions *more*, across all three genres. `[E]`

**Why it fails:** concealment is not the engine. Part of the effect is likely processing fluency: once the destination is known, the reader spends attention on the writing itself rather than on plot bookkeeping.

**Important caveat:** the effect held when the spoiler was presented *in advance and outside the piece*. A spoiler embedded mid-narrative did not produce the same benefit.

**Do instead:** put tension in *how*, not *whether*. The canonical demonstration is the opening of *One Hundred Years of Solitude*, which tells you a colonel will face a firing squad before it tells you anything else. Knowing the destination does not reduce the pull. It creates it.

**Corollary:** delete every "all will be revealed," "but first, some background," and "the answer will surprise you."

## 2. "Follow the story arc and readers will come"

**Killed by:** Boyd, Blackburn & Pennebaker (2020), *Science Advances*. Roughly 40,000 traditional narratives (novels, film scripts) plus about 20,000 non-traditional texts (science journalism, TED talks, Supreme Court opinions). `[M]`

**What they found:** a genuinely consistent three-part structure across narratives: staging, then plot progression, then cognitive tension. Fact-driven texts showed a different shape.

**What they also found:** **no evidence that adherence to normative story structure was related to the popularity of the story.**

**Do instead:** use the arc as hygiene. It keeps a piece coherent. It is not a growth lever, and any advice selling structure as the reason something went viral is unsupported by the largest study of the question.

---

## 3. "Open loops make it memorable" (the Zeigarnik claim)

**Killed by:** a 2025 systematic review and meta-analysis of the Zeigarnik and Ovsiankina effects. `[M]`

**What died:** the memory claim, that unfinished tasks are better recalled. It has resisted replication for decades and the meta-analysis found no memory advantage.

**What survived:** the **Ovsiankina effect**, a general tendency to resume interrupted tasks. Open goals pull.

**Do instead:** keep the mechanism, drop the justification. Open gaps because they create a pull to resume, which replicates. Never claim they make your writing more memorable, which does not.

---

## 4. "Short punchy sentences are more engaging"

**Complicated by:** Ashok, Feng & Choi (2013), EMNLP. Statistical stylometry discriminated successful from less successful books at up to **84% accuracy**. Successful works showed *greater* syntactic complexity, which the authors noted works against conventional readability. `[C]`

**Read this carefully.** It is correlational, the success proxy is download counts, and the authors say so themselves. It does **not** license writing dense prose.

**Do instead:** stop flattening. Vary shape deliberately. Do not shorten a clear sentence because short is assumed to be engaging. There is no evidence that it is, and the best available evidence points the other way.

---

## 5. "Be vivid"

**Complicated by:** a meta-analysis of vividness and persuasion whose own title calls the effects "elusive." Vividness does not reliably increase persuasion. `[M]`

Separately, prose memory is one of the tasks where concreteness effects are *not* robust, even though concreteness effects hold in many other memory tasks.

**Do instead:** pursue **concreteness**, calibrated. Concreteness means a name, a number, a date, a mechanism. Vividness means emotional intensity and sensory decoration. Concreteness has support; vividness does not. And concreteness has an optimum rather than a maximum: a meta-analysis of 8,977 headline experiments found that when a headline is too vague, more concreteness helps, but past a point more concreteness *reduces* clickthrough. `[M]`

## Also on the watchlist

**"Curiosity gaps work."** They do increase clicks. They also backfire. Overhyped promises that do not deliver produce distrust and defiance, and around 62% of users report distrusting sites with misleading headlines. A gap you do not close is not a technique, it is a trust liability. See `ethics.md` rule 1.

**"Rhetorical devices make writing harder to read."** No. McQuarrie & Mick (1996) and follow-up work found figures produced more elaboration and more favorable attitude *without being harder to comprehend*. `[E]`
