# Fiction

Peitho was built for prose that argues. Fiction runs on the same axiom and changes four things about how the rules apply. This file is the delta, not a second skill. Everything not listed here carries over unchanged.

**Tiering warning, stated up front.** The empirical corpus behind Peitho is about persuasion, comprehension, and engagement. Very little of it is about novels. Most of this file is `[L]`, craft lore held because it is useful and widely taught, and it is labeled that way rather than dressed up. Three findings do transfer, and they are marked.

## What changes

**1. The gaps become dramatic, not informational.**

The slot mechanic runs unchanged: every scene closes one gap and opens another. But in argument the gap is *what is true*, and in fiction it is *what will she do*, *what does he know*, *what does this cost*. The reader's participation is prediction and moral judgment rather than inference.

**2. The ethics rail changes shape without weakening.**

Rules 2 and 4, which forbid unsupported claims and invented concreteness, exist because a reader takes expository prose as a claim about the world. Fiction is not that claim, so invented detail is the medium rather than the violation.

What survives intact:

- **Real people, real places, real events, real history stay true.** A novel may invent a senator. It may not invent a quote from a living one.
- **Internal consistency replaces external truth.** The claim ledger in `coherence.md` becomes the continuity ledger. A character's eyes do not change color, and a three-day journey does not take one.
- **Author's notes, acknowledgments, and marketing copy are expository prose.** The full rail applies there with no exception.

**3. Concreteness inverts toward the sensory, and the inverted U gets sharper.**

The line-level rule still demands a concrete anchor per passage, but in fiction the anchor is sensory and specific rather than a name or a number. The inverted-U calibration matters more here, not less: the reader co-creates the image, so over-specification takes their job away. Describe the one detail that implies the room. Describing the whole room closes the slot.

**4. Register becomes characterization.**

The contraction default holds for narration by default, and it becomes a tool in dialogue. A character who never contracts is characterized by that. A character who contracts everything is characterized differently. Choose it per character and hold it. See the contraction default in `deslop.md`, including its ban on phonetically spelled dialect, which is a separate thing from register and reads as condescension toward the speaker.

## The em-dash exception, and it is narrow

Interrupted dialogue conventionally breaks on an em-dash, and no other mark does the job:

> "But I told you the bridge was—"

**Allowed:** interruption, in dialogue, in fiction. These do not count against the em-dash ceiling.

**Not allowed:** em-dashes in narration, em-dashes for emphasis or aside, em-dashes as a rhythm crutch anywhere. The ceiling applies in full to every line that is not an interrupted speech.

This is a real exception for a real typographic need. It is not a reopening of the law.

## The craft rules

All `[L]` unless marked.

- **One head per scene.** Head-hopping between characters inside a scene is the loudest amateur signal in fiction. Break the scene or stay put.
- **Scene for the turn, summary for the transit.** If something changes, write the scene. If nothing changes, summarize it in a sentence and move.
- **Show the decision, not the emotion label.** "She was furious" is a label. What she does next is the information. Cut the label and keep the act.
- **"Said" is invisible. Keep it.** Speech tags do not need variety, and adverbs in speech tags ("he said angrily") do the actor's work for them.
- **Characters get proper names, not epithets.** "The older man," "the blonde," "the taller of the two" are placeholder constructions that a reader has to translate on every appearance. Name them.
- **Cut any sentence that exists to be admired.** Purple prose is the writer stepping in front of the scene. The test is whether the sentence would survive if nobody could praise it.
- **No process narration, ever, in fiction.** Law 2 of `tells.md` applies with no exception here. There is no signpost case in a novel.

## What transfers from the empirical corpus

**Do not withhold the outcome to build suspense.** Leavitt & Christenfeld (2011) tested ironic-twist, mystery, and literary stories and found spoiled versions were enjoyed *more* across all three genres. `[E]` This is the most directly fiction-relevant result in the corpus and it contradicts the most common piece of fiction advice. Tension lives in how, not in whether. See `myths.md`.

**Structure is hygiene, not a growth lever.** Boyd, Blackburn & Pennebaker (2020) found the staging, progression, tension shape across roughly 40,000 narratives, and found no relationship between adherence to it and popularity. `[M]` Use the arc to keep a draft from sprawling. Never believe it buys an audience.

**Do not flatten prose in the name of readability.** Ashok, Feng & Choi (2013) found more successful novels ran *more* syntactically complex, against readability scores. `[C]` Correlational, so this licenses permission rather than a target. It does mean the "short sentences are more engaging" rule has no support behind it in fiction.

## Self-check for fiction

1. Every scene closes one dramatic gap and opens another.
2. One point of view per scene.
3. Continuity ledger checked: names, physical details, timeline, established facts.
4. Every em-dash is an interruption inside dialogue. Every other one is gone.
5. Emotion labels cut in favor of action.
6. Characters named, not described by attribute.
7. Real-world facts inside the fiction are accurate.
8. The ending closes the dramatic gaps. The closing method in `SKILL.md` applies unchanged.
