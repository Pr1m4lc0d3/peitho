# Form calibration

Two inputs set every decision in an opening: how fast the reader can leave, and how they feel about you before the first word.

## Exit speed

The hard number: Chartbeat, across two billion visits, found **55% of pageviews receive under 15 seconds of active attention**, and the average reader stops around halfway through an article. `[M]`

Faster exit means the visible gap must arrive sooner and the concrete anchor must land earlier. Nothing else about the doctrine changes.

| Form | Exit speed | Gap visible by | Concrete anchor by | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Social reply, post | ~1 second | first clause | first sentence | No runway at all. The gap and the anchor are often the same words. |
| Headline, subject line | ~1 second | the line itself | the line itself | Calibrate concreteness to the inverted U. Too vague and too specific both lose. |
| Landing page, ad | 2 to 5 seconds | first sentence | first sentence | Reader is defensive by default. Treat stance as hostile. |
| Blog post, article lead | ~15 seconds | first sentence | first paragraph | The 55% cliff lives here. |
| Newsletter | ~15 seconds | first sentence | first paragraph | Slightly receptive: they opted in. |
| README, docs intro | ~20 seconds | first sentence | first paragraph | Reader has a task. The gap is "will this solve my problem." |
| Essay, long feature | 30 to 60 seconds | first paragraph | first paragraph | Room for a scene before the claim. |
| Chapter, short story | 1 to 3 minutes | first paragraph | first page | Reader has committed money or time. Most runway available. |
| Book opening | minutes | first page | first page | Still the highest-stakes page in the book. |

Two rules that do not vary with form:

- Every paragraph, at any exit speed, closes one gap and opens another.
- Every concrete anchor is true. No exceptions at any speed.

## Audience stance

Classical rhetoric split openings by how the audience already feels. The distinction still holds.

### Receptive

They asked for this. Subscribers, buyers, someone who clicked your name.

Use the **direct opening** (*principium*). State the gap plainly. Do not perform reluctance or build up to your own topic.

### Neutral

They arrived by accident. Search, feed, a link.

Use the direct opening but front-load harder. Serve all three exordium jobs in the first two sentences: make them attentive, make them receptive, make them well disposed.

### Hostile or exhausted

They distrust you, disagree with you, or are simply worn out from reading.

Use **insinuatio**, the oblique approach. Do not open with the claim they will reject. Open on shared ground, a concession, a question, or a concrete instance, and let the claim arrive once they are inside.

Cicero named three causes of audience hostility. All three are ordinary now:

1. Something in the case is discreditable. *You are selling something.*
2. Previous speakers have already convinced them otherwise. *They read a competitor, or a bad take on your category.*
3. **The audience is wearied by listening.** *They are scrolling a feed and have read forty things today.*

That third cause is feed fatigue, described in the first century BC. For an exhausted reader, a frontal claim reads as one more demand. A concession or a concrete instance reads as relief.

**Worked example.** Orwell opens *Politics and the English Language* with a concession to the reader's likely position, then reverses it: most people would admit the language is in a bad way, but it is generally assumed nothing can be done about it. He grants the premise, names the resignation, and only then attacks the resignation. That is insinuatio, and it is why the essay does not read as scolding.

## Stance overrides speed

When the two conflict, stance wins. A hostile reader with 60 seconds of runway still needs the oblique approach. A receptive reader with one second still gets the direct one.
