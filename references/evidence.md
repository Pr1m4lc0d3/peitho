# Evidence

The corpus Peitho is built from. Every entry carries a tier tag, what it actually found, and what it licenses. Anything not verifiable to a primary source is marked as such rather than quietly used.

**Tiers:** `[M]` meta-analytic or replicated. `[E]` strong single study, named. `[C]` correlational only. `[L]` craft lore, no empirical support, kept because it is useful. `[?]` encountered secondhand, primary source not verified.

---

## Immersion and persuasion

**Green & Brock (2000).** "The Role of Transportation in the Persuasiveness of Public Narratives." *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology* 79, 701 to 721. `[E]`

Four experiments (N = 97, 69, 274, 258). Developed and validated the transportation scale. Higher transportation produced stronger story-consistent beliefs and more favorable evaluations of protagonists. Experiment 2 found highly transported readers detected **fewer false notes** in a story. Experiment 4 manipulated transportation directly via processing instructions; reducing it reduced belief change.

*Licenses:* immersion as the working mechanism. *Also obligates the ethics rail*, because the same finding means immersion suppresses reader scrutiny.

**van Laer, de Ruyter, Visconti & Wetzels (2014).** "The Extended Transportation-Imagery Model." *Journal of Consumer Research* 40(5), 797 to 817. `[M]`

Meta-analysis of 132 effect sizes from 76 published and unpublished articles. Systematized antecedents and consequences of narrative transportation in a consumer context.

*Licenses:* treating marketing prose and story prose as the same mechanism.

**Braddock & Dillard (2016).** "Meta-analytic evidence for the persuasive effect of narratives on beliefs, attitudes, intentions, and behaviors." *Communication Monographs* 83(4). `[M]`

Beliefs: k = 37, N = 7,376, r = .17. Attitudes: k = 40, N = 7,132, r = .19. Intentions: k = 28, N = 5,211, r = .17. Behaviors: k = 5, N = 978, r = .23. Moderator analysis on fictionality was mixed; neither medium nor research design moderated the effect.

*Licenses:* the honest ceiling. Effects are real and modest. Fiction and nonfiction behave similarly. Quote these numbers rather than implying anything stronger.

## Curiosity and the gap

**Loewenstein (1994).** "The Psychology of Curiosity: A Review and Reinterpretation." *Psychological Bulletin*. `[M]`

Curiosity as cognitively induced deprivation arising from a perceived gap in knowledge. Curiosity requires that attention be focused on the gap. It peaks with a small amount of knowledge and diminishes both when one knows too little and when one knows too much.

*Licenses:* the visible-gap rule, and both failure modes. Withholding everything gives no gap to see. Explaining everything removes the gap.

**Aristotle, *Rhetoric*.** The enthymeme. `[L-classical]`

A rhetorical syllogism that leaves a premise or the conclusion implicit, relying on shared *endoxa*. The missing element is meant to be supplied in the mind of the audience.

*Licenses:* the axiom. Pull is participation. This is the same mechanism Loewenstein described 2,300 years later.

**Zeigarnik and Ovsiankina, 2025 meta-analysis.** *Humanities and Social Sciences Communications*. `[M]`

Found **no memory advantage** for unfinished tasks. Found a general tendency to resume them. Concluded the Ovsiankina effect represents a general tendency while the Zeigarnik effect lacks universal validity.

*Licenses:* open gaps for the resumption pull. Never claim they aid memory.

## What actually moved live audiences

**Heritage & Greatbatch (1986).** "Generating Applause: A Study of Rhetoric and Response at Party Political Conferences." *American Journal of Sociology* 92(1). `[E]`

476 speeches at British party conferences in 1981. **Seven rhetorical formats were associated with close to 70% of the applause.** Contrasts alone accounted for 33.2% of collective applause incidents; lists 12.6%. The seven: contrast, list, puzzle-solution, headline-punchline, position taking, combination, pursuit.

*Licenses:* format selection from measured devices instead of lore. Note that this measures applause at political conferences, not reading behavior, so it transfers by analogy and is tagged `[E]` rather than `[M]`.

**McQuarrie & Mick (1996).** "Figures of Rhetoric in Advertising Language." *Journal of Consumer Research* 22(4), 424 to 438. Plus the 1999 visual follow-up. `[E]`

Framework separating schemes from tropes across four operations: repetition, reversal, substitution, destabilization. Figures examined (rhyme, antithesis, metaphor, pun) produced more elaboration and more favorable attitude toward the ad **without being harder to comprehend**.

*Licenses:* rhetorical figures at no comprehension cost. Kills "clever costs the reader effort."

## Structure

**Boyd, Blackburn & Pennebaker (2020).** "The narrative arc: Revealing core narrative structures through text analysis." *Science Advances* 6(32). `[M]`

About 40,000 traditional narratives plus about 20,000 non-traditional texts. Found a consistent three-process structure: staging, plot progression, cognitive tension. Fact-driven texts showed different structures. **No evidence that adherence to normative story structure related to story popularity.**

*Licenses:* the arc as hygiene. Explicitly forbids selling structure as a success formula.

## Calibration

**Chartbeat.** Analysis across roughly two billion visits. `[M]`

**55% of pageviews received under 15 seconds of active attention.** Average reader scrolls about halfway through an article.

*Licenses:* the exit-speed table in `forms.md`. Industry data rather than peer-reviewed, but the sample is enormous and it is the best available measure of real reading behavior.

**Headline concreteness meta-analysis.** "When curiosity gaps backfire: effects of headline concreteness on information selection decisions." *Scientific Reports* (2024). `[M]`

Meta-analysis of **8,977 headline experiments**. When a baseline headline is too vague, more concreteness raises clickthrough. When headlines are already too concrete, more concreteness *lowers* it. An intermediate amount of information maximizes clickthrough.

*Licenses:* concreteness as an inverted U, not a maximum.

**Vividness meta-analysis (2016).** "Revealing the elusive effects of vividness." *Social Influence*. `[M]`

Effects of vividness on persuasion are unreliable.

*Licenses:* pursuing concreteness rather than vividness. Related: concreteness effects are robust in several memory tasks but **prose memory is not one of them**.

## Endings and what a reader retains

**Alaybek, Dalal, Fyffe, Aitken, Zhou, Qu, Roman & Baines (2022).** "All's well that ends (and peaks) well? A meta-analysis of the peak-end rule and duration neglect." *Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes* 170, 104149. `[M]`

**174 effect sizes.** The peak-end effect on retrospective summary evaluation was large, **r = .581, 95% CI [.487, .661]**, and robust across the moderators examined. The peak effect was large; the end effect was medium. Both were stronger than duration, beginning, trough, trend, and variability effects.

*Licenses:* the exit module in `SKILL.md`. A reader's verdict is not the average of the piece, so a piece needs a deliberate peak placed late but not last, and an ending built rather than tacked on. Note the honest caveat: this measures retrospective evaluation of hedonic episodes, not of reading specifically. It transfers by analogy, and it is tagged `[M]` for the strength of the meta-analysis rather than for direct evidence about prose.

## Repetition

**Dechêne, Stahl, Hansen & Wänke (2010).** "The Truth About the Truth: A Meta-Analytic Review of the Truth Effect." *Personality and Social Psychology Review* 14(2), 238 to 257. `[M]`

**51 studies** of the repetition-induced truth effect. Repeated statements are rated more true. Within items **d = .39, 95% CI [.30, .49]**; between items **d = .50, 95% CI [.43, .57]**, random effects. The between-items effect is the larger of the two.

*Licenses:* law 3 in `tells.md`, and it is why that law is a distinction rather than a ban. Repetition is genuinely persuasive, so repeating a *term* is correct. Restating a *claim* in fresh vocabulary is the machine tell. Do not cite this to justify saying the same thing twice.

## Hedging

**Hyland (1998).** *Hedging in Scientific Research Articles.* John Benjamins. `[E]`

Systematic analysis of hedging forms across journal articles plus interviews with research scientists. Hedges are abundant in scientific writing and functional: they present a claim as plausible reasoning rather than certainty, and they express collegiality rather than presumption. Hyland's position is that the belief that professional scientific writing is a series of impersonal statements of fact is itself mistaken.

*Licenses:* the hedge budget in `tells.md`, and it is why that law is a budget rather than a prohibition. A writer who never hedges is overclaiming. The failure mode is undifferentiated hedging at uniform density, which is what a model does by default.

## Coherence

**Kintsch & van Dijk (1978).** "Toward a Model of Text Comprehension and Production." *Psychological Review* 85, 363 to 394. `[E]`

Models comprehension as the reader organizing meaning elements into a connected whole and condensing the text to its gist.

*Licenses:* treating coherence as structural work the reader performs, not as a stylistic nicety.

**Haviland & Clark (1974).** "What's new? Acquiring new information as a process in comprehension." *Journal of Verbal Learning and Verbal Behavior* 13, 512 to 521. `[E]`

The given-new strategy: a reader searches memory for an antecedent matching a sentence's *given* information, then attaches the *new* information to it. **Comprehension time was fastest when the antecedent was directly available and slowest when it had to be inferred.**

*Licenses:* rule 2 in `coherence.md`, given before new. This is a measured processing cost, not a preference, which is why it outranks the other coherence rules.

## Spoilers

**Leavitt & Christenfeld (2011).** "Story Spoilers Don't Spoil Stories." *Psychological Science*. `[E]`

Three experiments, 12 short stories, three genres (ironic-twist, mystery, literary). Spoiled versions were enjoyed **more** across all three genres. The benefit appeared when the spoiler was given in advance and outside the piece; a spoiler woven into the narrative did not reproduce it.

*Licenses:* tension in *how*, not *whether*. See `myths.md` entry 1.

## Style and success

**Ashok, Feng & Choi (2013).** "Success with Style: Using Writing Style to Predict the Success of Novels." EMNLP. `[C]`

Statistical stylometry discriminated more from less successful books at up to **84% accuracy**. Successful works showed greater syntactic complexity, running against readability. The authors state explicitly that this is correlation, not causation, and conjecture that conceptually complex work may require syntactic complexity.

*Licenses:* do not flatten prose on the assumption short equals engaging. Does **not** license deliberately dense writing.

## Register

**Biber (1988).** *Variation across Speech and Writing.* Cambridge University Press. `[E]`

Factor analysis of 67 linguistic features across a corpus of spoken and written English. The first and strongest dimension, "Involved versus Informational Production," separates interactive, affective, generalized text from dense informational text. **Contractions carry a positive loading on the involved pole**, alongside first and second person pronouns, present-tense verbs, private verbs, and *that*-deletion. Nouns, prepositions, longer words, and high type-token ratio load on the informational pole.

*Licenses:* the contraction default in `deslop.md`. Full forms are a register choice, not a correctness standard, and a piece written entirely in them sits at the distant end of the strongest measured dimension in English style. Note the limit: this describes register, not persuasive effect. No study here shows contractions persuade better.

**"Uniform full forms are a tell for machine-written prose."** `[L]`

Craft observation from editing, not a measured result. Held because it is consistently useful and it costs nothing if wrong: the register argument above already carries the rule.

## Classical rhetoric

**Cicero, *De Inventione*; *Rhetorica ad Herennium*.** `[L-classical]`

The *exordium* has three jobs: render the audience **attentive** (*attentos*), **receptive or teachable** (*dociles*), and **well disposed** (*benivolos*). *Captatio benevolentiae* is the move that secures the third.

**Insinuatio**, the oblique approach, is prescribed when the audience is hostile. Three named causes of hostility: something discreditable in the case; earlier speakers have already persuaded them otherwise; **the audience has been wearied by listening**.

*Licenses:* the three exordium jobs, and the stance table in `forms.md`. Tagged as classical doctrine rather than empirical finding, but note the convergence: Heritage & Greatbatch independently measured contrast and puzzle-solution as the top applause devices, and both are participation structures of exactly the kind classical rhetoric prescribed.

## Encountered secondhand, not verified

Do not cite these without checking the primary source first.

- **"62% of users distrust sites with misleading headlines,"** attributed to a 2024 Pew study in a secondary source. `[?]` The direction is well supported by the clickbait literature; the specific figure is unverified.
- **"Curiosity-driven titles boost clicks 22%,"** attributed to a 2025 *Journal of Consumer Psychology* report in a secondary source. `[?]`

The general finding that clickbait raises clicks while damaging trust is robust across multiple sources. Use the finding, not these numbers.
